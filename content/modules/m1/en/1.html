Climate change has reduced the productivity of major crops in many regions of the world [13], which may cause great problems by the year 2050, when the population on Earth should reach about 9.8 billion. With the estimated population 30 years from now and decreasing productivity of agriculture, farmers will be challenged to secure global food security [21] and if farmers want to meet the upcoming increase in demand for meat and dairy products when those demands lead inevitably to even further use of natural resources which may affect the climate, they will need to adjust their farm management, for example, sowing dates, the use of intermediate crops, and the use of more resistant crops to a specific climate [23].

No agriculture system is immune to climate change. Even though there are genetically improved plants and management practices that give farmers improvements in yields and sustainability, climate change may easily offset all of this mentioned above [8]. For example, climate variability is the main cause of changes in yields in the Midwestern United States [8]. The instability of yields may result in economic swings for consumers and producers. After all, this can be seen by looking 10 years back when the drought in 2012 caused a 53% increase in the price of corn relative to the previous 5-year average price [2].

At this time, we can see a decrease across the world in soil fertility, as well as carbon sequestration, microbial activity, and crop growth. Those and other negative effects on our environment are supported by the low diversity and high intensity of inputs that farmers put into agroecosystems. Another problem, one that is also disturbing, is the predicted increase in the occurrence of drought, intense precipitation, higher temperatures, and contamination of soils by salts and heavy metals when all of this will inevitably lead to increased occurrence of pests and pathogens, which will take its toll on crop yields [15].

Climate changes are significantly affecting agriculture and food production. Climatic conditions such as wind, drought, high temperatures, and floods have a great impact on food safety and poverty reduction. However, this is especially the case in highly water-dependent countryside communities. With upcoming climate changes, lower yields from staple crops are expected due to lower productivity [9].

Climate Resilient Agriculture

Climate resilient agriculture is a form of farming that aims for the sustainable use of natural resources that are at our disposal through crop production systems. The main goal is to increase the productivity of farms in the long term and at the same time while maintaining the income of the farm under climate variabilities.

Climate resilience is the ability to keep up with agricultural structures while ensuring their functions with constantly changing climatic conditions. Farmers can support climate resilience by improving the robustness, adaptability, and transformability of agricultural practices [16]. Robustness is meant the capacity of the agricultural system to be able to deal with climate extremes. Adaptability is meant as the capacity to change agricultural practices like marketing and risk management to reduce the impacts of climate extremes while maintaining the structure of the system. Transformability is meant the capacity to change the structure and function of the system in response to climate change [16]. Overall, the aim of improving climate resilience does not include only increasing the productivity of crops while the climatic conditions are changing but also improving the ecosystems and their biodiversity while minimizing environmental degradation [20].

Climate resilient cropping system

Climate resilient crops (CRCs) were bred for a specific purpose, to cope with biotic and abiotic stress, for example, drought, where there are numerous ways how to fight drought through water management. For example, water-friendly technologies like a furrow irrigated raised bed, rainwater harvesting, cover crop method, micro irrigation, etc. When drought occurs, and with it high temperatures, a relatively safe way how to get satisfactory yields is the use of different kinds of technologies based on crop water estimation, adoption of water conversation methods, changing the fertilizer and watering schedules, etc.

For example, the drought-resistant varieties of rice grown in India have yielded approximately about 1t/ha higher than common varieties when undergoing drought stress. Rice is not the only crop that is affected greatly by drought or floods. Maize and wheat are also sensitive to these stresses. Drought is responsible for up to 20% loss in yield in maize. Drought-tolerant varieties of Maize can yield up to 2 – 3t/ha than common varieties that will yield up to 1t/ha in the same conditions [25].

Another reason why CRCs were bred is water resistance. For example, the introduction of short-duration varieties of pea, soybean, wheat, and sorghum in India led to increased yields significantly [24]. It is important to say that a large part of the territory where these crops are grown suffers from submerging due to floods, and almost 80% of the rice-growing territory in Eastern India is suffering from excess water or drought, depending on rainfall pattern and thus making farmers completely dependent on the environment. By using a flood-tolerant rice variety, that can manage up to 17 days of complete water submerging and with a yield of 3t/ha, farmers can reduce their dependency on environmental factors [22].

There are more reasons why to use CRCs, such as high temperatures, salinity, and shorter vegetation period, as well as to cope with pests. Another purpose why CRCs were bred is to improve water retention in soil, improve soil quality, and erosion reduction. Climate resilient crops (CRCs) have a higher tolerance to biotic and abiotic stresses compared to commonly grown crops [3].

The main purpose of these crops is to maintain the yield under stress conditions compared to crops that are commonly grown. Thanks to the properties CRCs have, farmers will get the means to adopt their farm management and sustain quality, yields, and financial stability. Climate-resilient crop systems aren’t only about using water, temperature, or salt tolerant varieties of crops, but it goes beyond the issue of biodiversity, alternative farming systems, conversation agriculture, and more [4].

Despite the benefits CRCs provide, adoption by small-scale producers ought to be mentioned, but in some cropping systems, it might not be as high as expected [6], [14]. Due to high risk and profitability concerns caused by switching to new and different ways of managing an agroecosystem, farmers are more likely to be timid in accepting and trying different ways, even though they know that these methods are more sustainable and environmentally friendly than their current approach.

Alternative farming systems

At this time, there are a few ways how farmers can manage their fields other than conventionally. For example, they can use alternative agricultural systems that are better than conventional farming as far as climate resilient crop system is concerned [17]. Alternative management systems may include no-till, reduced tillage practices, crop rotation, the use of cover crops, decreasing usage of inorganic inputs, etc. Research shows that these alternative systems of farming might have the potential to mitigate environmental pollution and greenhouse gas emissions while improving water and soil quality [1].

Current knowledge suggests that climate changes will affect biotic (pests, pathogens) and abiotic (solar radiation, water temperature, water) factors in crop production, which will lead to threatening crop sustainability and production [14]. That is why farmers should also focus on biodiversity and try to diversify their agroecosystems because more diverse systems will perform better in changing environments. Conversion agriculture

Conversion agriculture is defined by FAO (Food and Agriculture Organization) as an approach to managing agroecosystems for improved and sustained productivity while increasing profits and food security and, at the same time, preserving and enhancing the resources and environment. 3 basic principles constitute, along with other complementary practices, conversion agriculture [11].

  • Principle 1 is about minimal tillage or no-till, which goes hand in hand with the practice of no-till sowing and direct sowing of planting material into untilled soil. This causes minimal soil disturbance from any operation.

  • Principle 2 is about the maintenance of a permanent mulch cover on the ground surface, which means the farmer needs to implement retaining crop biomass on the field.

  • Principle 3 is about the diversification of crop species, which requires to use of annuals and perennial crops, cropping system rotation, and the right mix of legume and non-legume crops.

These three principles are the foundation of conversion agriculture, which means if a farmer is applying one or two of these principles in his agroecosystem, he isn’t conversion agriculture compliant. The purpose of conversion agriculture is to increase biological biodiversity and biological processes in agroecosystems. Tillage shouldn’t be used, or it should be at least reduced to an absolute minimum. External inputs, like inorganic and organic fertilizers, should be applied optimally and in quantities that will not affect biological processes in soil [11]. While being compliant with all principles of conversion agriculture, farmers will be able to improve overall farm management, mainly in raid-fed areas. With other well-known practices, for example, integrated pest, water, and nutrient management, conversion agriculture are the base system farmers should choose if they want to intensify their production while keeping it sustainable [10]. The main reasons to accept conversion agriculture are better farm economy due to a reduction of inputs, like water, fertilizers, agrochemicals, time, and fuel, flexible technical possibilities for fertilizer application and weed control, equal or higher yields, greater yield stability [12], higher protection of soil from water and wind erosion [19], higher fertilizer retention and higher water retention with better water management in drought areas [5].
