Agriculture leaves a significant footprint on the earth's surface. According to the United Nations (FAO), arable land is approximately 1.53 billion hectares, which is about 12% of the earth's land. Grasslands cover approximately 3.38 billion hectares, which is about 26% of the earth's land. This combined area of agricultural land represents about 38% of the earth's surface, making agriculture the industry that uses the largest amount of land on the planet. The remaining land is occupied by deserts, mountains, tundra, cities, ecological reserves, and other areas unsuitable for agriculture [1]. In recent decades, global crop production has increased substantially along with agricultural land. FAO reports a 47% increase in production between 1985 and 2005 (ref.18), while the increase in agricultural land is only 2.4%. However, despite the impressive increase in crop yields between 1985 and 2005, between 1965 and 1985, the increase in production was even 56%, which may indicate that crop yields are increasing more slowly today than before, and it is a matter of time before yield growth completely stops [1]. However, one of the ways to constantly improve plant yields is to focus on the biodiversity of the agroecosystem. Biodiversity is the basis of agriculture, as it includes all species of plants, animals, and microorganisms within the agroecosystem [2]. Biodiversity provides valuable genetic resources for further crop breeding. Crops that are used today have ancestors in wild species or varieties that have been selectively crossed or hybridized [2]. Perry [3] stated that biodiversity has an essential role in the provision of ecological services, such as preventing soil erosion and/or microclimate regulation. Altieri [4] added that biodiversity contributes to ecosystem services by recycling nutrients or regulating pest populations. However, these ecosystem services can only be preserved or maintained if biodiversity is preserved in the agroecosystem [4]. If there is a loss of biodiversity in an agroecosystem, very high maintenance inputs can be expected, simply because the loss of biodiversity in an agroecosystem will cause the system to lose its self-sustaining capacity.

One of the ways to maintain biodiversity, or to further improve it, is reduced tillage. Currently, it appears that changes in soil cultivation and crop rotation practices have a great influence on the composition of soil fauna, including pests but also useful organisms. Tillage has a significant impact because it changes the chemical and physical properties of the soil. Research by Stinner and House [5] shows that the response of pest populations to reduced tillage varies quite a bit. Reduced tillage indirectly shapes the species composition of the soil microbiome by contributing to soil moisture retention and modulating soil temperature [6]. The species composition of the microbiome associated with reduced tillage will create an environment that can be antagonistic to pathogens through competition [7].

Environmental Implications of Plough Tillage

Intensive tillage and the use of heavy machinery have produced mixed opinions at best in today's knowledge [8]. Soil erosion has become a global problem with increasing demand for primary soil resources and is a major risk to agricultural productivity and sustainability. As such, plowing exacerbates wind and water erosion, which is becoming an increasing problem today as more and more emphasis is placed on agricultural intensification. Plowing disrupts the soil structure and exposes it to intense rainfall and high wind speeds, which directly leads to erosion [8].

Erosion is a very significant cause of soil degradation, along with others, such as soil carbon loss, nutrient depletion, soil compaction, soil acidification, or salinization [9]. The problem of erosion, especially the water one, is not only about the washing away of topsoil, which in itself represents a big problem, but water erosion has other negatives, such as the floating of pesticides into the groundwater, which can infect water courses and threaten these ecosystems as well.

In recent years, reduced tillage practices have become increasingly widespread, which have the potential to increase soil mass, improve carbon sequestration, and thus potentially contribute to mitigating climate change [10], [11], [12]. Reduced tillage results in an increase in the amount of organic matter and less compaction of the soil, which results in improved physical properties and thereby, for example, improving the ability of the soil to absorb precipitation and retain water, which can later be used for crops [13], [14], [15].

Crop Rotation, Cover Crops, and Precrops Effect

Soil health depends on a complex community of interacting organisms, including bacteria, fungi, protozoa, arthropods, and earthworms. Plant residues are the source of nutrition for these organisms. Microbially mediated decomposition of plant residues releases energy and nutrients, enabling these soil organisms to provide essential ecosystem services [30], [31].

Studies comparing organic and conventional farming practices have been conducted to understand the dynamics of the soil food chain and to determine whether organic matter inputs have advantages over inorganic sources, which currently represent the largest inputs to agricultural land [32], [33], [34], [35]. Supporting the development of resilient systems is increasingly important today, if only because the livelihood of communities depends on these systems [1].

Resilient systems, improved soil health, and other extremely important factors in tillage can be improved through crop rotation. The definition of crop rotation is a systematic or repetitive sequence of crops grown over several harvest seasons. The choice of crops is mainly influenced by the ecology of the agroecosystem and the economics of agricultural production [36] and is not determined by the farmer, or at least he should not determine it. Crop selection depends on edaphic, biotic, and climatic factors. Pearson [36] introduced the term "comparative advantage", where this designation is related to the adaptation of a crop to the environment, differences in the necessary inputs to produce a given crop and market influences. This can directly affect sowing practices and, therefore, crop rotation because if the economic advantage is high enough for several crops, the farmer will prioritize this and, therefore, not grow monocultures. Cover crops are part of climate resilient systems. These are defined as plants grown primarily to cover soil to protect it from soil erosion and nutrient loss [37], [38]. Cover crops can be classified in both temperate and tropical climates [39], [40]. Cover crops are recognized for their ability to contribute to various agroecosystem services [41]. Agroecosystem services are complex concepts involving various interacting elements that are spatially and temporally interconnected, making it challenging to separate the effects of individual elements from the overall system [42]. Agricultural ecosystems, intended primarily for the production of food, feed, fiber, and industrial raw materials, receive and provide agroecosystem services [43].

Crop rotation has been a recommended practice for plant disease control [44]. Different crop species grown in sequence disrupt disease cycles. For example, in Australia, planting narrow leaf or blue lupine reduced common root rot infection rates in wheat compared to continuous wheat monoculture [45]. Crop rotation is particularly effective for nematode control. In Alabama, rotation with corn resulted in consistent increases in soybean yield compared to continuous soybean under conventional tillage, primarily due to the treatment of soybean cyst nematodes [46]. Tillage practices can interact with crop rotation in disease management. In some cases, conservation tillage systems combined with crop rotation have resulted in improved disease control and increased yields compared to continuous cropping and conventional tillage [46], [47]. However, the interactive effects of rotations and tillage systems on disease organisms may differ [48], [49].

Cover crops are primarily grown to absorb nitrogen from the soil, effectively reducing nitrogen loss through leaching, a form of water erosion [51], [52]. Intercropping efficiency is closely related to the depth of root systems rather than root density [51], [52]. When green manure crops are integrated into the soil through tillage, they often accelerate the mineralization of organic nitrogen. This acceleration can lead to a temporary mismatch between the release of mineral nitrogen, which becomes prone to losses, and the actual nitrogen demand of crops. Such asynchrony can come from a variety of sources, including the addition of inorganic nitrogen fertilizers, animal manure, green manuring, or natural mineralization of soil nitrogen.

In some cases, cover crops exhibit preemptive competition, taking up nitrates from soils that would otherwise be at risk of leaching and making them available to succeeding crops [53]. Preemptive competition is observed, especially during the late spring growth of non-legume cover crops and in regions characterized by a low risk of leaching, such as dry areas. In such situations, optimizing intercropping strategies over several years can be beneficial in terms of maximizing N availability for cash crops.

Conclusion

In conclusion, agriculture has a significant impact on the earth's surface; cultivated land covers approximately 12% of the planet's surface, and pastures occupy about 26%. This combined agricultural area represents about 38% of the earth's surface, making agriculture the largest land-using sector. However, despite substantial increases in crop production over the past few decades, there are signs that crop yields are growing more slowly than before. Limited tillage practices have gained importance as a means of improving soil health and mitigating environmental problems associated with intensive tillage, such as soil erosion and carbon loss. Crop rotation, cover crops, and pre-crop selection are essential components of climate-resilient agricultural systems. Crop rotation breaks disease cycles and can improve overall soil health. Cover crops, on the other hand, help reduce nitrogen loss through leaching and can provide several ecosystem services. Considering growing concerns about climate change and sustainable agriculture, it is increasingly important to adopt agricultural practices that promote soil health, reduce environmental impacts, and increase the resilience of agricultural systems. The integration of limited tillage, strategic crop rotation, and the use of cover crops is a promising approach to achieving these goals, ultimately contributing to more sustainable and climate-resilient agriculture. However, it is essential to realize that the effectiveness of these practices may vary depending on local conditions and must be adapted to specific agroecosystems to achieve the best results.
