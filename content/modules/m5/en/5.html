In previous chapters, it was already mentioned that livestock production is a significant source of greenhouse gas emissions, participating 14.5% of total anthropogenic emissions [1]. Greenhouse gases (GHGs) retain heat in the Earth's atmosphere, leading to rising temperatures and other climate changes. Therefore, mitigating greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions from livestock is crucial to combat climate change and reducing the associated risks, environmental degradation, protecting ecosystems and biodiversity, improving public health and economic stability, and promoting a more sustainable and equitable future for all. While adaptation efforts are also crucial, mitigation remains the most effective way to address the root causes of these challenges, as the livestock sector is a significant contributor to global emissions. Regarding this, the EU strategy entitled "carbon farming" is gaining more and more importance in recent years. It focuses on managing both land and livestock through the management of soil carbon stocks, materials and vegetation, greenhouse gas (GHG) flows at the farm level, with the aim of mitigating climate change. "Carbon farming" includes practices and changes in land use that produce one or more of the following outcomes: 1) carbon capture (sequestration) and subsequent storage in biomass above/underground and in agricultural land; 2) avoiding future CO2 and other GHG emissions; and/or 3) reduction of existing CO2 and other GHG emissions.

Since livestock GHG emissions vary significantly between production systems and regions, Gerber et al. [1], believe that the potential for mitigation lies in the gap between management practices resulting in the lowest and highest emission rates. By implementing practices currently used by the top 25% of producers with the lowest emission intensity, emissions from the livestock sector could be reduced by 18%. Furthermore, if techniques employed by the top 10% of producers were adopted, emissions could be reduced by 30%.

The animal production industry is responsible for anthropogenic greenhouse gas emissions, but at the same time, it has significant potential for mitigation. Contemporary scientific literature provides a wealth of data on possible measures to mitigate climate change from the aspect of animal husbandry. In order to briefly present the main of them, a sumarize of mitigation strategies by Grossi et al.[2], is given in Table 1.

Land Resource Management

Land resource management can be key to mitigating the environmental impact of livestock. Mitigation strategies that rely primarily on improvements in land management are thought to be far more effective than those that primarily focus on reducing emissions from livestock [3]. The same authors suggest that switching to more efficient and less demanding livestock systems can significantly reduce emissions. For example, it is known that raising cattle in feedlots results in lower emissions compared to pasture systems. However, by applying adequate measures in the management of pastures and livestock production, it could contribute up to 7% of the global mitigation potential from the impact of agriculture by 2030 [4].

Strategies that could be implemented include practices based on the principles of regenerative agriculture and carbon farming. The mentioned practices basically rely on carbon sequestration.

Carbon sequestration is the process of capturing carbon dioxide from the atmosphere and storing it in the land. It has been estimated that soil under crops and pastures can store up to 8.6 gigatons of carbon dioxide per year [5]. Therefore, improvements in the management and utilization of agricultural areas (pastures, fields, etc.) that result in effective carbon sequestration in the soil can mitigate the impact of livestock systems on the environment. By applying different agricultural practices - commonly called "regenerative agriculture" and "carbon farming" - it is possible to create new or increase existing carbon sinks:

-practices to increase productivity: fertilization, irrigation, use of productive perennial grass species, presence of legumes

-production of crops with higher yields, which can reduce the need for additional land use and help protect existing forests and grasslands. This can also lead to increased carbon sequestration, as more carbon is stored in plant biomass and soil.

-converting arable land into grasslands and improving the floristic composition of grasslands contributes to carbon sequestration by improving soil health and promoting the growth of plants with deep roots that can store more carbon in the soil. This can additionally have a positive effect on reducing erosion and improving water quality.

-conservation-reduced tillage (shallow ploughing), which reduces soil degradation during crop production and prevents excessive release of carbon into the atmosphere.

- application of practices used in organic and sustainable agriculture, such as covering crops, applying compost, etc. which are in principle close to the principles of regenerative agriculture

-reduction of deforestation due to their significant role in the process of carbon sequestration and ensuring the ecological balance

- The application of "silvopasture" as a form of agroforestry is the practice of grazing domestic animals on areas with integrated trees within the framework of cultivated fodder plants. In addition to the benefits of trees on grassland fertility and soil carbon sequestration, feeding tannin-rich leaves can reduce methane emissions in ruminant guts [6].

-improving the management of grazing lands has the potential to capture approximately 0.15 gigatonnes of carbon dioxide equivalent per year on a global scale [8].The application of the rotational grazing system, reflects favorably not only on the preservation of pasture quality, but through higher productivity and better root growth, a greater accumulation of organic matter (carbon) is ensured. In addition, sequestration on such pastures can be increased by applying measures of regenerative agriculture, by adding compost, for example. Here, however, it is important to point out that the sequestration effect achieved in this way is not permanent but is measured over years or decades. It is certain, therefore, that at some point the gas emission will exceed its deposition in the soil and this grazing system will again become a source of emission

-sustainable management of agricultural residues can bring double benefits - increasing the carbon sink through the decomposition of plant residues on the soil and reducing the emission of greenhouse gases through the use of the remaining biomass for the production of bioenergy instead of fossil fuel [9].

Enteric Fermentation Management

Reducing methane emissions from ruminants is a significant challenge in the ruminant production sector. Various strategies have been suggested to address this issue, which can generally be divided into four categories:

1) dietary manipulation

2) feed additives

3) vaccines and

4) genetic selection

These options have different levels of effectiveness in reducing methane emissions, as shown in Table 1. The focus is on improving rumen fermentation efficiency and increasing animal productivity to reduce methane emissions.

Dietary manipulation

The quality and digestibility of forage have a significant impact on enteric methane production. Along with the maturation of plants, there is an increase in the content of lignin, which reduces their digestibility. Harvesting forage, particularly grass, at an earlier stage of maturity for ensiling can increase the soluble carbohydrate content and reduce lignification. Practices aimed at improving forage quality have shown a potential reduction in enteric methane of about 5% per unit of fat protein corrected milk, according to Knapp et al. [10]. Physical processing of forage - chopping, grinding and steaming, also improves their digestibility and alleviates methane production in the intestines of ruminants [11]. However, the potential of such emission reduction is not significant, i.e. is less than 2% per unit 4% m.m. corrected milk [10].

Increasing the proportion of concentrate in ruminant rations to improve diet digestibility is another effective strategy for mitigating methane emissions. According to Knapp et al. [10], this approach can significantly reduce methane emissions - as much as 15% per unit of 4% m.m. of corrected milk with a proportion of concentrate of 35-40%. However, simultaneously with the increase in the proportion of concentrated nutrients (fermentable carbohydrates) in meals, the risk of developing metabolic diseases such as rumen acidosis also increases [1].

Therefore, it is important to carefully consider the ratio of bulk and concentrated nutrients when implementing this strategy.

Addition of fat or fatty acids to ruminant diets can reduce enteric methane emissions by reducing the proportion of energy from fermentable carbohydrates and altering the microbial population in the rumen [12]. Some by-products such as cottonseed, brewer's grains and cold-pressed canola meal have been proven effective in reducing enteric fermentation [13]. Dietary lipid supplementation at a level of 5% to 8% of dry matter intake is an effective strategy for mitigating methane emissions, as reported by Grainger and Beauchemin [14]. In this way, the emission of enteric methane can be reduced by about 15% per unit of 4% m.f. corrected milk [10].

However, the mitigation potential of high-oil by-products is still understudied. In some cases, methane production may increase due to increased fiber intake [11]. Inclusion of lipids higher than 10% can disrupt rumen function by altering the microbial population, which reduces fiber digestibility. This potentially leads to increased excretion of organic matter and nutrients, as well as methane emissions from manure. Addition of lipids can also adversely affect rumen fermentation, milk fat synthesis and fatty acid composition. Meals with a higher content of oil and oilseeds reduce feed intake and fiber digestibility, so they are generally not recommended in the diet of younger and fattening ruminants. On the other hand, their use can be stimulating in lactating animals [15;16]. When considering the use of oil for emission reduction, it's important to take into account that the production of oilseeds is associated with a significant increase in upstream GHG emissions per kg of dry matter [16].

Feed additives

The most commonly used food additives with the ability to modify rumen biochemical processes in the direction of methanogenesis suppression are phytobiotics, probiotics, organic acids, and ionophores [17; 18]. Although a significant number of previous studies have shown the effectiveness of their use, for wider application, more extensive research is needed to rule out side effects such as possible toxicity and associated health risks.

The inclusion of secondary metabolites from medicinal and aromatic plants (MAPs) in the diet of ruminants leads to a significant modulation of the rumen microbiome, favoring the growth of beneficial microorganisms and inhibiting methanogenic microbes [19]. These so-called phytobiotics alter rumen fermentation patterns by stimulating the synthesis of propionate and other volatile fatty acids, while limiting the hydrogen required for methanogenesis. This metabolic redirection further contributes to the reduction of methane emissions. Finally, certain metabolites act antimicrobially, which is another direct way of inhibiting methanogenesis [20].

The most significant secondary metabolites present in plants that are important for reducing methane emissions are: essential oils, saponins, tannins, polyphenols and flavonoids.

Еssential oils can affect fermentative processes in the rumen resulting in reduced CH4 production in vitro. Some studies have given promising results with a reduction of methane emissions up to 90% [21]. Essential oils are thought to remodel the gut microbiome in a way that reduces methanogenic microorganisms [22]. The suppressive effects of certain extracts and essential oils can be attributed to their toxic nature, which leads to a decrease in the population of microorganisms in the rumen, microbial fermentation and methanogenesis [23]. The mechanism of this action is still not completely clear, so for the adequate use of essential oils in the suppression of methanogenesis, it is necessary to conduct more extensive research in in vivo conditions.

Other bioactive compounds in plants - saponins, tannins, polyphenols, flavonoids, etc., mainly act as a mechanism of inhibition of methanogenic microbes in the gastrointestinal system of ruminants. Thus, saponins inhibit proteolytic ciliated protozoa and, through defaunation, indirectly contribute to the reduction of CH4 emissions. Tannins exert their action by inhibiting hydrogen-synthesizing microorganisms, as well as by suppressing methanogens [17]. It is considered that the potential of flavonoids in mitigating CH4 emissions is also significant, but more extensive research in in vivo conditions is necessary.

Starting from saponins as defaunerators of protozoa and tannins as methano-inhibitors and suppressors of H2 production during fibrolysis, some research has addressed the effects of these plant compounds on methanogenesis in ruminants. A study by Antonius et al.[23], showed that supplementation of M. oleifera, C. longa Linn., and P. timoriana in ruminant diets effectively increased total gas production, digestibility of dry matter and organic matter, and reduced CH4 gas emissions and protozoan populations during rumen fermentation.

Other bioactive compounds from plants, such as polyphenols and flavonoids, may also be useful in an anti-methanogenesis strategy in the rumen. In vitro study by Jo et al. [26], showed that adding extracts (containing polyphenols and flavonoids) from oriental MAPs (Vitis vinifera L. and Rhus succedanea L.) to ruminant diets can reduce methane production and the presence of methanogenic archaea.

A study by Rossi et al. [27], showed that a combination of essential oils, bioflavonoids and tannins reduced methane production by 8 to 22% in vitro. Optimum product concentrations for 24 hours were 0.0025-0.005% DM in liquid form. In an in vivo study, this mixture was observed to improve milk production, feed digestibility and feed conversion rate. These findings suggest that natural products such as essential oils, bioflavonoids, and tannins have the potential to reduce methane production while improving dairy cow performance. Further validation in in vivo trials could lead to a reduced environmental impact of lactating cows.

Seaweeds contain compounds (polysaccharides, proteins, peptides, lipids, phlorotannins, saponins and alkaloids) that reduce CH4 production by suppressing archaea and protozoa. Experiments in which cattle were fed algae showed a noticeable reduction in methane emissions while simultaneously improving production performance. Among the compounds found in algae, bromoform (CHBr3) exhibited the highest level of effectiveness in inhibiting methanogenesis [23].

Organic acids are commonly incorporated into domestic animal diets for multiple reasons. They enhance the acidity of the feed, preventing spoilage such as in silage. This improved pH level stimulates digestion and nutrient absorption, while also maintaining a healthy balance of microorganisms in the digestive system [23]. Formic acid, when used at lower doses, reduces overall gas production, but higher doses can have the opposite effect [28]. Both formic acid and fumaric acid exhibit significant inhibitory effects on gas production.

Ionophores are commonly used as additives in livestock feed to improve utilization efficiency and body weight. These compounds act as CH4-inhibiting factors promoting the growth and proportion of Gram-positive bacteria in the rumen increasing propionic acid. Studies have shown that the use of ionophores, especially monensin, can lead to significant reductions in CH4 production in livestock. Holstein cows treated with monensin showed a 24.3% decrease in CH4 production [29] while in Angus steers there was a 30% decrease in enteric CH4 production [30]. The CH4-reducing effect of monensin is attributed to changes in the production of volatile fatty acids in the rumen. It is important to note that excessive ionophore supplementation can be toxic to ruminants and that their residual levels in animal products should be monitored.

Probiotics are additives based on bacterial and/or yeast cultures. Introduced into the body, they achieve many benefits (on health, reproduction, production) and are increasingly being considered as a tool in the fight against methane emissions through the manipulation of the gastro-intestinal microbiome. The mechanism of their suppressive effect on methanogenesis is based on the fact that they direct fermentative processes towards the production of propionate. This reduces the concentration of hydrogen as a key precursor for methane synthesis.

In a recent in vitro experiment, Chen et al. [31], found that certain strains of propionic acid bacteria (Propionibacterium jensenii LMGT2826, Propionibacterium thoenii LMGT2827 and Propionibacterium thoenii T159) reduced CH4 emissions by 18%, 8% and 20% compared to the control.

Vaccines

The goal of such vaccines is to trigger the animal's immune response and the creation of antibodies with a suppressive effect on methanogen activity. After the synthesis of antibodies in the animal's blood and saliva, they continuously pass through the saliva into the rumen, where they exert their effect. After vaccination, the mechanism of antibody synthesis once initiated should provide a suppressive effect on methanogenesis for a long period. The use of vaccines to reduce CH4 production in ruminants has been suggested as a promising strategy, but its effectiveness is difficult to evaluate due to the lack of comprehensive studies. The variety of methods used in different studies also makes comparison of results challenging. Further research is needed to determine the feasibility, practical implementation, and sustainability of this strategy, including considerations such as antigenic capacity, Igs in saliva, and how to accurately measure CH4 production [32]. According to the researchers' current estimates, the first vaccines are not expected to be administered before 2030.

Genetic selection

The use of genetic selection as a strategy for methane emission reduction could be briefly defined as the selection and favoring of individuals with a desirable genetic code in terms of reduced methane production and emission. This strategy might not give quick results, but in the long run it can be very effective because it allows for cumulative and lasting changes. The problem in its application primarily lies in the fact that it is necessary to generate databases on methane emissions in animals, while currently only a few countries actively record them. Additionally, comprehensive genomic reference databases as well as precise genetic indicators for CH4 emission traits need to be developed [16]. In previous research, a higher heritability of methane emission traits was found in sheep (0.24–0.55) compared to dairy cows (0.11–0.33).

When choosing traits that would be emission indicators, the most suitable are: infrared (IR) spectrum of milk, rumination time, feed efficiency and rumen microbiome composition [33]. Recent research on a large sample of over 200,000 cows has confirmed that the wavelengths of the IR spectrum of milk are heritable [34]. This indicates that it would be possible to use genetic covariance between informative spectral wavelengths and methane emissions. Accordingly, milk volatile fatty acids have also been proposed as potential indicators for methane production. Another trait that has attracted significant interest as a potential indicator for emission reduction is the rumen microbiome. Difford et al. [35], conducted a study on 750 Holstein cows using 16S rRNA ribotyping and found that certain bacteria and archaea were associated with methane production and that there was significant heritability. Another study using nanopore technology to assess the whole metagenomes of 334 dairy cows revealed genetic correlations between methane concentrations and different genera of protists, archaea, anaerobic fungi and bacteria. The mentioned studies emphasize the potentially important role of the rumen microbiome in understanding and mitigating methane emissions. Ten years of research by Rowe et al. [36], confirmed a significant correlation between genetic material and CH4 yield in sheep. Genetic selection, i.e. favoring genotypes with lower methane emissions, resulted is economically advantageous: ewes with a lower CH4 yield (12% difference), weaned heavier, meatier lambs with a higher wool yield.

In considering genetic selection as a strategy to reduce CH4 emissions, it is important to consider its effect on productivity. It was found that with increasing productivity, emission intensity decreases, but this effect is more pronounced in low-productive individuals [18]. However, the selection of low-emission animals should also take into account the possible impact on feed utilization efficiency, especially in terms of NDF digestion, which is important in feed production. Factors such as digestion rate, feed rate, rumen microbiome and rumen fermentation can influence both feed utilization efficiency and CH4 emissions [16].

Unilateral selection to reduce methane emissions is unacceptable from the point of view of animal husbandry. Therefore, the question of its relationship with other traits that are of selection importance arises. Based on current knowledge, it is unlikely that selection for reduced methane emissions in ruminants would have significant effects on other traits such as reproduction and health. However, there is evidence of a relationship between methane production and milk production, as well as dry matter intake (DMI). Additional analyzes on larger datasets are necessary to further explore and confirm relationships with other traits [37].

In general, the use of genetic selection as a tool for mitigating strategies, although promising, does not allow for quick application and results, so it is currently more important to focus on other strategies with faster effects along with intensive work on generating conditions for its application.

Manure Management

Manure contributes to GHG emissions because its decomposition releases methane and nitrous oxide. The emission level largely depends on the environmental conditions, its management and composition [38]. Methane and nitrogen oxide emissions are influenced by organic matter and nitrogen content in excreta.

Methane is produced when organic matter in anaerobic conditions is incompletely decomposed under the influence of bacteria. This most often happens when liquid manure is stored or treated in lagoons. Methane production can be additionally enhanced by long storage as well as warm and humid environmental conditions.

Nitrogen oxide is mostly produced from solid manure, its storage or deposition on pastures in a combination of aerobic and anaerobic conditions.

GHG emissions are generally higher with solid manure systems. Numerous studies have shown that the application of various practices can contribute to the reduction of emissions from manure. Some of the most important practices are as follows:

Dietary manipulation is a practice that has found its application in emission reduction not only directly - by modulating enteric fermentation, but also indirectly it can preferably affect the composition of manure [39]. It is essentially the effect of a protein-balanced diet on denitrification and N2O emission, which reduces nitrogen excretion through excreta. Also, diets enriched with tannin from natural sources may increase the proportion of nitrogen excreted in organic compounds via faeces, rather than in the urine as urea, which in turn reduces the potential for NH3 and N2O emissions [40].

Manipulation of the storage temperature is based on preventing the development of methanogenesis, which is favored by higher temperatures. Therefore, manure cooling can be a cost-effective way to mitigate emissions, especially in colder regions. The effect will depend on the frequency of manure removal from the barn and the temperature difference between the environments. In some holding systems that allow active cooling of the manure in the manure chute, cooling the manure at 42 ˚C was found to reduce CH4 emissions by 16% compared to the non-cooling situation [41].

Composting of solid manure is a process of aerobic decomposition of its organic matter under the influence of microorganisms to carbon dioxide and water. This process can be enhanced by turning the manure and aerating it. Methane emissions can be reduced by aeration, but it can also raise the emission level of CH4, N2O and NH3 [42].However, with enhanced composting (overturning and aeration), lower emissions of CH4 and N2O are achieved compared to ordinary passive composting.

Covering solid manure during storage with straw or plastic film generally leads to a reduction of N2O and CH4 emissions, and can also have a positive effect on NH3 emissions [42].

Covering liquid manure during storage is primarily aimed at preventing NH3 emissions. The danger of N2O emissions during the storage of liquid manure is small, except in cases of formation of scum on the surface. The scum increases the potential for nitrification and denitrification, which results in N2O emissions, because with the drying of the scum, there is an intrusion of O2 [43]. Similarly, scram formation increases the potential for methane oxidation as well. Lids and straw are used to cover the liquid manure, although the straw itself is a form of protection. Although all covering methods result in lower overall GHG emissions, the best results are achieved by combining covers and straw.

Manure separation is a process of mechanical separation of its liquid and solid fraction. To achieve the desired effect of emission reduction, additional measures are necessary for each of the fractions in the process of their storage. This includes the cover options mentioned above, while anaerobic digestion is also recommended for solid manure.

Anaerobic digestion is a technological procedure by which easily degradable organic matter in manure and other organic substrates is transformed into biogas (mainly CO2 and CH4). In this way, a double benefit for the environment is obtained - the use of energy from biogas instead of fossil fuels and the reduction of CH4 emissions during the storage of digested manure, which is further used as fertilizer. It is estimated that the total effect of this method in reducing GHG emissions exceeds 30%.

Manure aeration can result in a significant reduction of total GHG emissions, ranging from 43% to 99% for beef and pig manure respectively [44;45]. However, N2O emissions can increase by as much as 144% [44], which is why in the application of this procedure it is necessary to implement measures to preserve N2 in order to achieve the full effect of GHG mitigation.

The treatment with additives is aimed at changing the chemical properties of the manure, most often the pH value, in order to prevent or reduce GHG emissions. In this sense, acidification with sulfuric acid of a certain concentration is most often used in order to lower the pH value of the substrate. Reduction effects range from 67%-87% for CH4 emissions in cattle to as high as 94%-99% in pigs [43].
