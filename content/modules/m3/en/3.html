Measuring the Amount of Water Consumed by Irrigated Crops

Due to the increase of the population and climate change, the demand for freshwater resources has been considerably increasing, the irrigations depriving other industrial sectors of water resources. Thus, it is very important focusing on ways to make the use of water more efficient for sustainable agricultural production. Such practices comprise at least the following optimal irrigation scheduling (which makes use of smart irrigation systems, controlled remotely, coupled with monitoring systems for soil, plants, and water, etc.) [1]. Precision agriculture provides solutions for different issues related with the resources used by farmers, including freshwater for irrigations. Conventional irrigation systems do not care so much about the changes in soils and weather changes that determine the evapotranspiration, leading sometimes with a larger than needed quantities of water, fertilizer leaching, surface runoff, ponding, deep percolation, plant stress. Knowing when and how much water to apply, is directly determining the efficiency of the water use. To do that, a deep understanding on the water use of the plants is necessary, with deep connections to the characteristics of the soil and climate conditions. In this framework, determining the amount of water consumed by crops is a crucial element. This can be done by the following techniques [2]:

  1. using lysimeters

  2. evaluating the water balance

  3. evaluating the soil moisture depletion

The total water consumed from vegetation covered soil includes both evaporation from the soil and evapotranspiration of plants. At the surface of the soil, water evaporation depends on environmental factors (saturation deficit, temperature, radiation, wind) which determine the atmosphere requirements for water, and factors depending on the soil (soil humidity, the presence or absence of water sources, properties related to the movement of the water in soil). For agriculture, the evaporation of the water from the soil is unproductive.

General Considerations Related to the Evapotranspiration

Soil water is also consumed through evapotranspiration which contributes to the formation of the vegetal mass of the crops. The biomass incorporates a little over 1% of the water consumed through evapotranspiration, the rest of the water is translocated from the soil through the plant contributing to different physiological processes such as regulation of the temperature, absorption, assimilation, etc. The transpiration depends mainly on the soil moisture and the water requirements of the atmosphere. If the soils are well provided with water, the intensity of transpiration is regulated according to the water content of the atmosphere, but if the soil does not have enough water, the transpiration rate decreases rapidly. At high water requirements (over 3 mm per day) the transpiration rate decreases even if the soil has good moisture, and the process is done by the reduction of the size of stomata. Of course, the transpiration depends on the type of the plants and its development state, as previously discussed in the chapter regarding crop water needs.

In agricultural practices, an important parameter is the total amount of water lost by evapotranspiration, ET. Generally, in temperate climate conditions in the northern hemisphere, the highest water consumption is recorded in July when most crops have maximum biomass, the soil and air temperatures are high, and nebulosity low. The average daily consumption strongly varies depending on the dynamic of plant growth, the succession of the vegetation phases and climate condition changes, especially the temperature. In spring crops that have long vegetation, the daily consumption increases in time reaching the maximum value during the critical phases for humidity - flowering, forming of the seeds, ripping – when the plants are fully developed and maximum transpiration. These phases also correspond with high evaporation needs of the atmosphere due to increased temperature, saturation deficit of water vapor, and wind. After the critical phases of the plant growth, the total water consumption decreases.

Evapotranspiration can be expressed using several quantities:

Potential evapotranspiration (PET) represents the amount of water that would be evaporated and transpired by a specific crop, soil or ecosystem if there is enough water available. It defines the water consumption in soil when its moisture is closed to field capacity and the land is covered with an active and well-developed vegetative cover of about 15-20 cm. In this state, nor the soil moisture nor the development stage of the plants limit the evapotranspiration which mainly corresponds to the evapotranspiration requirements of the atmosphere.

Maximum evapotranspiration (MET) represents the water consumption of the soil when it is well provided with water and the plants (a specific culture) covers the land. In this situation, the plant is the only factor influencing the evapotranspiration.

Optimum evapotranspiration (OET) represents the water consumption, between the field capacity and minimum moisture content which favor obtaining an economically efficient crop, and the plants are in the vegetative development stage. In this case, the influence of the soil moisture on the evapotranspiration is negligible; the only limitation comes from the water consumed by the plant depending on the needs during the respective vegetative state.

Real evapotranspiration (RET) expresses the water consumption in normal conditions, meaning when the soil moisture corresponds to the normal local meteorological conditions. Its value is influenced by the soil moisture and by the plant as well.

Evaluation of Irrigation Water Needs

To determine the irrigation water needs, direct and indirect methods can be used. Direct methods make use of field calculations based on the evaluation of the water balance in soil using parcel method or lysimeter vessels [3].

The method of optimal irrigation of a parcel of land is a reference to determine the water needs of an irrigated crop, and consists of establishing some irrigation parcel of lands where an optimal irrigation regiment is adopted. In this way, all through the vegetation season, the optimum moisture is ensured in the soil on a depth corresponding to the size of the roots of the culture. The dynamics of soil moisture between seeding and harvesting of a specific culture is followed closely, with measurements made every ten or fourteen days, and after precipitation or irrigation events. The irrigation water needs are determined and expressed as the average daily consumption (in m3/ha⋅day) using the following equation:

OET = Ri + Pv + M − Rf

Where OET is the water consumption during the studied time frame, Ri is the water reserve in soil at the beginning of the time interval, Pv represents the precipitation during the considered time frame, M is the sum of irrigation quantities or the supplementary amount of water distributed to the plants to maintain the soil moisture between the field capacity and the minimum moisture content, and Rf represents the water reserve in soil at the end of the considered time frame.

This method has the advantage of allowing a precise estimation of the irrigation needs of a specific crop. It also has some drawbacks due to the high volume of work necessary to sample and measure the moisture, as well as the impossibility of a precise evaluation of water leaks mostly through percolation under 1.5 m depth.

The lysimeter method uses some parallelepipedal or cylindrical vessels, with a surface of 1 to 4 m2 and the depth of 0.8 – 1.2 m, made of a metal sheet of 2 – 3 mm thickness or concrete. The bottom has a drain which allows the excess water to be collected. On the bottom of the lysimeter there is a drainage layer of 30 – 35 cm on which the soil is placed to cultivate a specific crop for which the water needs are determined.

Lysimeters are placed in the field, in batches of 3 for each crop, within an irrigated parcel of land of about a quarter of a hectare to ensure the same microclimate as the crop. All operations are made in the same conditions as for the field crop. A schematic of a lysimeter is presented in Figure 1.

The necessary of water in the lysimeter is ensured either from periodical wetting or from a reservoir with constant level placed over the drainage layer through the principle of communicating vessels. For periodical wetting, which is the easiest way to provide the necessary water, the time interval of applying is usually 5 days. Wetting is applied such as to ensure slightly higher water amounts than the retention capacity of the soil. The extra water evacuated through the drainage at the bottom of the lysimeter is measured and recorded. Soil moisture before starting the crop and at harvest time are measured from soil samples. In this case:

MET = Ri + Pv + M − Rf − D

where all the parameters as those previously mentioned and D represents the amount of water from drained from the lysimeter.

The estimation of irrigation needs through the lysimeter method allows a precise calculation of the maximum evapotranspiration on short time spans allowing the monitoring of water needs variations during different vegetation stages, critical phases and as function of special meteorological conditions such as very high temperature, heavy rains, and so on.

Indirect methods are based on the dependance of the plant water consumption on some climatic factors, are operative and economical with reasonable results. The connection between crop water needs and climate factors is expressed through empiric formula that are used to determine the evapotranspiration. Some of these indirect methods are also based on measuring the evapotranspiration using atmometers or evaporimeters. The most appropriate formula proposed by Thornthwaite expresses the potential evapotranspiration as function of the average monthly air temperature, multiplied with a correction coefficient depending on the latitude:

$$PET = 160 \bullet \left( \frac{10 \bullet t_{n}}{I} \right)^{a} \bullet k_{\phi}$$

where PET is the potential evapotranspiration, tn is the average monthly temperature for which the PET is calculated, I the annual thermal index of the area, equal to the sum of monthly thermal indexes $I = \sum_{n = 1}^{12}i_{n}$, $i_{n} = \left( \frac{t_{n}}{5} \right)^{1.514}$ is the monthly thermal index (for negative monthly average temperatures, the monthly thermal index is considered zero),

The method based on measuring the evapotranspiration is largely used for determining the irrigation needs. The evapotranspiration is measured using Piche and Bac A class evaporimeters. To determine the OET, the evapotranspiration rates are multiplied just as the values calculated based on the Thornthwaite equation, with empiric correction coefficients, specific to a crop, month and climate area. The coefficients are established based on following the water consumption (through the optimum irrigated parcel method) in parallel with estimating the evaporation using evaporimeters and making the ratio of the two average consumption values, depending on the crop and on some specific time intervals:

$$K_{p} = \frac{OET}{E_{p}}$$

where Ep is the water collected in the same interval in an evaporimeter. Using such devices to estimate the water consumption of crops has advantages, because these are simple instruments that can be made with low costs, on one hand, and are easily maintained without the need of a specialist.

The Piche evaporimeter is presented in Figure 3. It consists of a graduated tube with a length of 30 cm, filled with distilled water and inverted on a disc (3 cm diameter) made out of filter paper. The device can be hung from a stand. It measures the evaporation rate of the water from the surface of the paper disc that is kept wet constantly. The readings are made daily at the same time.

Bac class A evaporimeter (Figure 4) is composed of a cylindric vessel of 120.65 cm diameter and 25.40 cm height, made out of hard metal sheet of 1.5 mm thickness, painted in white color. It is placed on a wooden grid of 5 x 10 cm, with a bitumen treated plank placed over a horizontal well compacted soil. The evaporimeter is placed under a rectangular cage (16 x 16 m) where perennial plants are cultivated, always cut at the height of the vessel. The readings are done inside a metallic cylinder with a micrometric device having a 0.02 mm precision. This cylinder has the role to ensure higher precision of the readings, without any errors generated by waves. At the beginning of the season, the evaporimeter is filled with clean water, 5 cm under the top of the vessel to avoid water losses and provide a reserve for the water accumulated from precipitations.
