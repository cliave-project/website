After the use of fossil fuels all over the world, we encountered a problem that has become increasingly influential. Especially with the century we left behind, global warming has entered our lives as an important problem due to the increase in greenhouse gases in the atmosphere.

In fact, the world's climate has changed many times throughout history. Most of these climate changes have resulted from changes in the amount of solar energy the planet receives due to small changes in the earth's orbit.

The rate of greenhouse gases in the atmosphere started to increase with the industrial revolution that started in the middle of the 18th century and reached values approaching 400 ppm today (Figure 1). The increase in the carbon dioxide ratio is largely due to the use of fossil fuels. Another important factor in this increase is the change in land use, especially deforestation.

Global Carbon Cycle

Carbon is an essential element for life. The movement of carbon in inorganic and organic forms between the atmosphere, biosphere, hydrosphere and pedosphere is called the carbon cycle [1]. The carbon cycle is a system that supports all life. Carbon in the atmosphere is bound by photosynthesizing organisms and then passed to animals through the food chain. These creatures give off CO2 to the atmosphere by respiration, or when they die, the carbon in their bodies accumulates in the soil or water.

Throughout the world, carbon is stored in different forms and areas (Figure 2). The largest stock of carbon on earth is in the continental plates and upper mantle of the world. Afterwards, the largest stock is found in the oceans, the vast majority of which is in the form of inorganic dissolved carbon and is not subject to change. There is only 900 Gt of carbon in the oceans for exchange. Carbon in the atmosphere is in the form of CO2 and contains about 839 Gt of carbon. It is estimated that the soils, together with the frozen soils, store around 3,500 Gt of carbon in total [2].

Soil Organic Carbon

Soil organic carbon is the part that remains in the soil after the wastes of living organisms have been partially decomposed. Soil organic carbon is the main component of soil organic matter and is the energy source of the soil. Organic carbon, which plays a major role in the regulation of basic soil properties, plays an important role in maintaining soil fertility. The loss of organic carbon in soils is an important indicator of soil degradation.

After the oceans, terrestrial ecosystems are the largest carbon storage areas. Carbon is not only stored in plants on land, it is stored in the soil in greater amounts. Therefore, even small changes in soil organic carbon mass can have profound effects on atmospheric CO2 concentration and therefore on climate change. There can be significant differences in the distribution of stored carbon depending on factors such as climatic conditions, land structure and use, and environmental factors. For example, the amount of carbon stored above ground is higher in the wet and warm parts of the tropics than underground. Soil organic carbon is estimated to be deposited with the largest amount of 190 Pg in areas of mostly peat soils located in northern latitudes. The carbon stored in these areas is largely found in the first 30 cm of soil. Low biological activity and slow decomposition of organic matter have resulted in the accumulation of large amounts of carbon in the soils of this region. This type of soil is often called Histosol and contains about 12-18% organic carbon [4]. On the other hand, in dry and hot regions such as the Sahara Desert, the vegetation cover is low and the carbon entry into the soil is very limited. Arenosols , the typical soils of these areas , often contain less than 0.6% soil organic carbon [5].

Global soil organic carbon (SOC) mass is related to the carbon cycle. A modeling based on current estimates (HWSD: Harmonized World Soil Database) gives a total mass of 2476 Pg globally [6]. The value obtained by correcting for the bulk density of HWSD of organic soils and especially Histosols equals a mass of 1062 Pg (Figure 3).

Changes in Carbon Stock

The amount of carbon stored in the Earth's atmosphere is very small compared to the carbon stored in the oceans and land (Figure 2). Both the amount and direction of carbon moving between different carbon pools can have a major impact on climate change. According to the measurements taken from the ice cores, the CO2 concentration, which was known to be around 280 ppm in the pre-industrial period, has increased over 380 ppm today, and the most important share in this increase was due to the use of fossil fuels. In the 1960s, it was already found that only some of the CO2 emitted accumulates in the atmosphere, while the remainder is retained on Earth. However, after 1980, it became clear that the terrestrial biosphere is also an important actor in the global atmospheric carbon balance, like the oceans [7]. It has been understood that land use transformations, especially deforestation, cause significant terrestrial carbon losses to the atmosphere.

Fossil fuel consumption is accepted as the driving force of climate change [9]. Human-induced (anthropogenic) carbon emissions have affected not only the atmosphere but also ocean and land stocks. In fact, the carbon storage of the oceans and terrestrial vegetation has increased over the past two centuries. The oceans take in 2.3 Gt of C carbon each year [10]. Similarly, the net rate of atmospheric CO₂ uptake by terrestrial biomes has nearly doubled compared to the 18th century, exceeding 2.6 Gt C per year. Currently, there is a net accumulation of 4 Gt C per year in the atmosphere, despite the annual removal of about 5 Gt C from the atmosphere via the ocean and land [2]. This increase in atmospheric CO₂ will continue to cause increases in CO2 levels, resulting in reduced oxygen availability and increased acidity in the oceans, and thus harmful climate feedbacks [11]. On the other hand, it is expected that there will be an increase in CO₂ and CH₄ emissions stored in frozen soils due to melting. There is a potential for an increase in terrestrial climate-carbon feedbacks as a result of increasing frequency and intensity of extreme climate events [12].

There is a decrease in soil organic carbon in areas that have been converted from forest areas to agricultural lands. Under these conditions, the cover layer consisting of vegetal residues at the top of the soil disappears and the source of organic matter disappears. With the cultivation of the soil, the decomposition of organic matter is accelerated and it becomes open to transport due to erosion. The disappearance of forest areas means that these areas, which act as a carbon sink, become carbon sources. It is claimed that 2.1 billion tons of CO2 emissions occur annually due to the degradation of tropical forests, of which 53% is due to timber production, 30% to firewood and 17% to forest fire [13].

Soil degradation which is both the cause and the result of climate change, is defined as the long-term decrease and loss of soil value and biological productivity, and according to estimates, the earth's soil surface has been degraded by 7-40% due to human causes [14].

Mangroves, which consist of trees and shrubs in coastal areas and are important carbon sinks for the world, are constantly decreasing. 40% of mangrove areas, which have suffered significant loss in recent years, are under threat of extinction [15]. Mangroves can store up to 25.5 million tons of carbon per year [16] and provide the global oceans with more than 10% of the required organic carbon [17]. It is estimated that between 0.15 and 1.02 billion tons of carbon is released annually due to degradation of mangroves, swamps and seagrass meadows [18].

In regions close to the poles, more soil is exposed with the melting of glaciers and snow, and this causes more heat to be absorbed by the soil instead of reflecting and returning the rays, thus causing a more warming effect. According to a scenario created by modeling, during the 21st century, an increase of 1.4–1.8 Wm−2 in the amount of absorbed shortwave radiation on a global scale was determined due to the reduction of the North Sea ice cover and the Antarctic Ocean ice cover [19].

Due to increasing urbanization, changes are seen in land cover, while biodiversity decreases, soil degradation increases. As a result, loss of biomass and carbon storage occurs. If current trends in population density continue, the urban area is expected to increase by 1.2 million km2 by 2030. This increase will lead to significant habitat losses, especially in areas that were relatively less affected by urban expansion. Within the pan -tropics, the loss of vegetation biomass from areas with a high probability of urban expansion is estimated to be 1.38 PgC (0.05 PgC year-1), which is close to 5% of emissions from tropical deforestation and land use change [20].

The Importance of Carbon Retention in the Soil

Carbon sequestration is the transfer of CO2 in the atmosphere in such a way that it cannot be re-released into the atmosphere in a short time and can be stored in soil organic carbon pools for a long time. It is a phenomenon that has attracted a lot of attention recently in order to be less affected by the negative effects of climate change by reducing the CO2 emission towards the atmosphere. There is general agreement that carbon sequestration in soils is technically feasible and has significant potential. It is estimated that 0.90 to 1.85 Pg of carbon per year can be captured in cultivated areas worldwide [21].

Carbon capture mechanism

Carbon stored by the plants forms a cover on the soil with the dead plant parts that return to the soil. The organic materials in this cover layer can decompose and return to the atmosphere in the form of CO2 and mix with the soil to a significant extent. In this way, organic carbon is stored in the soil.

The main source of soil organic carbon is organic matter that has been mixed into the soil by the decomposition of dead vegetation. The decomposition of dead vegetation occurs with two events called oxidation and humusification [22]. Organic materials in the litter become mineralized by oxidation if the conditions are suitable. As a result of oxidation, carbon turns into CO2, hydrogen into H2O, nitrogen into NO2- and to NO3-, sulfur to SO3-2 and SO4-2, and phosphorus to PO4-3 [23]. Meanwhile, the plant nutrients contained in the organic matter are also released [24]. In the absence of the necessary conditions for decomposition, oxidative decomposition is limited. In this case, the humification event, which progresses more slowly and ensures the decomposition of organic matter, comes into play [23]. These events take place in three stages. First, high polymer compounds are broken down into building blocks. In the second stage, the plant tissues are partially consumed by the animals in the soil and decomposed. In the final stage, the decomposed and shrinking organic residues are changed by microorganisms. While the most easily degraded parts are water-soluble substances such as carbohydrates and pectin, the cellulose and lignin-rich parts take more time to decompose [24]. The part that emerges as a result of decomposition loses its textural structure and turns into amorphous and colloidal humus, which part of the plant is unknown [23]. As a result of advanced decomposition, humic substances appear in the soil. Humic substances with a large surface width are found either adhered to each other or bound to clay minerals and oxides [24]. Some compounds in the structure of organic matter, such as amino acids, carbohydrates and some organic acids, are called non-humic substances. The classification of the organic matter in the soil as humic and non-humic is related to whether the carbon, which forms the basis of soil organic matter, is stable or unstable [22]. Unstable soil organic carbon is the part of organic matter that decomposes in a short period of time, while stable soil carbon can take thousands of years to decompose. CO2 flow between soil and atmosphere is directed by oxidation of unstable soil organic carbon [25].

Effect of Land use change in carbon capture

Land use pattern is of great importance in soil carbon sequestration. Grasslands and forests generally sequester carbon at a higher rate than arable land. Among these categories, it is expected that the carbon balance of the soil will be affected depending on the changes in land use (Table 4). Soil organic carbon tends to decrease when woodlands, grasslands or other local ecosystems are converted into arable farmland. On the other hand, when the opposite is the case, there is an upward trend in soil organic carbon [26].

There are many studies that follow the status of soil organic carbon content in land use changes.

The effects of land use changes on soil carbon stocks were tried to be revealed by Guo and Gifford [27] with a meta-analysis by reviewing a large number of literature. According to the results, soil carbon stocks decreased in conversion from rangeland to plantation ( -10% ), from natural forest to plantation (-13%), from natural forest to field (-42%) and from pasture to field (-59%). In addition, an increase in soil organic carbon content was observed in areas converted from natural forest to pasture (+8%), from field to pasture (+19%), from field to plantation (+18%), and from field to forest (+53%).

In a study in which different study results were compiled in order to reveal possible soil carbon changes when forest areas were converted into agricultural areas, it was revealed that the soil carbon content was lost on average by 30% with the conversion from forest to agricultural lands [28].

In a study examining the effects of afforestation, especially on carbon storage in the soil, Pinus radiata fields were created where grain and vine were grown in the past. The results, in which soil carbon storage was determined by modeling, were shared by establishing radiata pine plantations [29]. After the afforestation of the old vineyards, carbon increases were observed in the top 5 cm of the soil, while the nitrogen contents remained the same or decreased. While most of the organic matter accumulation during afforestation took place in the upper soil layers, annual carbon accumulation per square meter was found to be 19 g in vine-grown areas and 41 g in grain-grown areas. In the study, a carbon sequestration potential of 950 and 700 g.C.m2 in 20 years was estimated for vine and grain, respectively, using RothC and Century models.

Increasing carbon capture in agricultural fields

While meeting the increasing and diversifying food demand in the world, it is of great importance to protect the existing agricultural lands and to provide this demand without encouraging the opening of natural areas to agriculture. In addition, increasing the productivity in existing agricultural areas and taking measures to minimize the losses in soil organic carbon while increasing agricultural productivity imposes responsibility on people in order to minimize the effects of climate change.

The term "carbon sequestration in soil" refers to the removal of atmospheric CO2 by plants and the storage of fixed carbon as soil organic matter. The main strategy in combating climate change is to increase the organic carbon density in the soil, improve the depth distribution of carbon and stabilize the carbon. In this context, managing agro-ecosystems correctly is an important strategy to increase soil organic carbon sequestration.

In particular, the wrong use of the land and the wrong soil management practices cause the depletion of organic carbon with the release of CO2 and other greenhouse gases into the atmosphere, while strengthening the organic carbon pool is a powerful tool to balance greenhouse gas emissions.

When degraded soils and ecosystems are restored, marginal agricultural lands are rehabilitated, plantations are established with perennial plants, and agricultural practices are adopted in line with the recommendations (Table 5), the sequestration capacity of soil organic matter for atmospheric CO2 can be significantly increased. Although the suggested agricultural practices are generally similar (mulch applied agriculture, reduced tillage, integrated plant nutrition practices, integrated agricultural control practices, precision farming techniques), specific practices for each area are extremely important in practice.

In line with the practices summarized in Table 5, if soil disturbances caused by tillage are eliminated, erosion losses are minimized, and large amounts of root and above-ground biomass are returned to the soil, more carbon will enter the soil. These practices conserve soil water, improve soil quality, increase productivity, and improve soil organic carbon pooling. With the transport of organic carbon to the subsoils, the retention time of carbon in the soils is extended.

Soil biodiversity has a positive effect on soil organic carbon. Ecosystems with high biodiversity hold more carbon than ecosystems with less biodiversity. Grassland, pasture and forest ecosystems have more carbon biomass than farmland [30].

Some of the soil and crop management practices that improve the soil organic carbon pool are briefly discussed below.

Conservation tillage

Conventional tillage and erosion result in the depletion of organic carbon pools found in agricultural soils. Therefore, the carbon storage capabilities of the soils will be increased if the soil is switched to non-tillage agriculture or if protective tillage methods are applied to minimize soil degradation.

Use of cover crops

It is important to include cover crops in crop rotation in order to increase soil organic carbon sequestration. The use of cover crops is a sustainable method to reduce the effects of climate change. With the use of cover crops, nitrogen and carbon can be added to the soil, nitrate leaks are reduced and the soil is protected from erosion [5]. In plant rotations; The use of cover crops, especially during fallow periods, is an important way to increase carbon input into the soil with the help of biomass production. If the plants to be used are selected among leguminous plants, they will add a significant amount of nitrogen to the soil.

With cover crops, nitrate leaching can be reduced in two ways. On the one hand, plants absorb the nitrate in the soil, on the other hand, they reduce the effect of infiltration by using soil water that will cause infiltration. It has been found that non-legume cover crops reduce the amount of NO3 leaking into groundwater resources by an average of 56 % [31].

Plant nutrition and irrigation applications

A correct plant nutrition management in agricultural areas is very important for soil organic carbon sequestration. In general, the use of organic fertilizers and compost will improve the soil organic carbon pool more than the use of inorganic fertilizers. When the amount of organic carbon in the soil falls below 1%, soil health is adversely affected and productivity decreases. On the other hand, increasing the amount of organic carbon in the soil is also a way of increasing carbon sequestration, as it increases the capacity of agro-ecosystems to absorb atmospheric CO2 and store it in the soil [32]. A 41-yearlong study was conducted in a paddy field in India and the effects of organic fertilizer applications on soil organic carbon content were tried to be determined [33]. As a result of the study, while 36 Mg C was found in 1 hectare area in the control plots where organic fertilizer was not applied, the amount of organic carbon was found to be 46.3 Mg with an increase of 29% in the application where chemical fertilization was combined with animal manure.

On the other hand, if irrigation is done by considering the plant requirement, as in fertilizer application to the soil, an increase in plant biomass will occur and the amount of organic carbon entering the soil will increase in this way.

Meadow pasture management and forest lands

Globally, restoring degraded grazing lands is important for soil carbon sequestration. Increasing the carbon sequestration in the soil can be achieved by some measures such as transforming the marginal areas used in agriculture into pastures, using fertilizers in the pastures, controlled grazing, and choosing legumes suitable for the region in the pastures. It has been reported that 0.11-3.04 Mg C can be retained annually in one hectare area by improving the pastures and the average amount of carbon captured will be 0.54 Mg [34].

Similarly, converting some of the marginal areas used in agricultural production but degraded to forests or perennial plant plantations can be a useful way to improve the soil organic carbon pool. Correct planning is of great importance here. In some studies, it has been reported that decreases in organic carbon content may occur in such applications. Afforestation of a cemetery with radiata pine (Pinus radiata) in New Zealand has been reported to decrease the organic carbon content of the soil by 15% [29].

Potential of earth's soils for carbon sequestration

130 Pg of carbon is stored in the upper 30 cm of soils in farmlands (Table 6). North America, Eurasia (Russia) and Europe regions account for more than 50% of all organic carbon stocks in cultivated land around the world. In contrast, the region of Central America, North Africa and Australia has slightly more soil organic carbon than 4.6% of the global total. South America has 9.42 Pg C despite having a sizable amount of farmland . Russia is the country with the highest carbon stock in the world, with 21.9 Pg C in cultivated areas, with 17% as a country. This is followed by the United States (18.9 Pg C), China (8.4 Pg C), India (6.4 Pg C) [21].
